I’ve been 3D printing for a while, and I’ve always been fascinated by how quickly the technology continues to evolve. From experimenting with different materials and print settings to designing and bringing my own projects to life, there’s something incredibly satisfying about turning a digital model, or an idea in my head, into a physical object.

When I first came across the Snapmaker U1, what immediately caught my attention was its approach to multi-filament 3D printing. Rather than relying on traditional filament-changing systems that can generate significant amounts of waste and increase print times, the U1 takes a different approach. Its tool-changing system promises to make multicolour and multi-filament printing faster, more efficient, and considerably more accessible.

Having recently picked one up myself, I wanted to document my experience, from unboxing and setup to my first prints and day-to-day use.

## Background 

I bought my first 3D printer 3 years ago on my birthday, as a gift to myself. The printer? a Creality Ender 3 V2. I was able to bag it for 150 bucks at the time, which was a really good deal.

It was, and probably still is, one of the best beginner 3D printers in my opinion. Now you may ask, but how? It doesn't have automatic bed leveling, or filament runout, or anything really except for the bare essentials to get something printed. Some may see it as a flaw, but I see it as the perfect opportunity to actually learn and have fun tinkering with the machine, rather than laying back and let the machine think for me. If I got a suped up printer to begin, I wouldn't learn how to manually level a print bed, or print upgrades to make my life easier, or buy and install modifications for the printer.

I feel starting with this printer was a great way to let my engineering side shine. As time went on, I found myself needing to print more demanding filaments and complex models, some upgrades I installed include;

- Klipper with Raspberry Pi Zero 2W
- Direct Drive Extruder
- mini Satsuna 
- Capricorn PTFE Tube

Many of these upgrades allowed me to start printing PETG/PLA+, monitor prints, and add macros. However, as time went on, issues started creeping in, I hit certain barriers that were impossible to overcome without serious financial investment, or, the easier option, just buying a new 3D Printer.

### Why now?

After three years with the Ender 3 V2, moving to something like the U1 feels like a pretty significant jump. I’ve gone from manually levelling a bed, tweaking firmware and troubleshooting the occasional failed print to having a machine that handles much of that process itself.

But what really interested me wasn’t necessarily the automation or the advertised print speeds. It was the tool-changing system.

Having previously used single-extruder printers, multi-colour printing was something I’d largely avoided. Traditional filament-changing systems require purging material every time a colour change occurs, which can result in an enormous amount of wasted filament, particularly on prints with lots of colour transitions. It’s also something that can significantly increase print times.

The U1’s approach is quite different. With four independent toolheads, each loaded with its own filament, the printer can physically switch between extruders rather than continuously unloading and reloading filament through a single nozzle. This is a particularly interesting solution, and one I was eager to try out for myself (after watching 100s of reviews).

## U1 First Impressions

Straight out of the box, the U1 feels like a substantial upgrade from what I’ve been used to. The overall design is considerably more enclosed and refined than my Ender 3 V2, and it’s immediately apparent that this is a machine designed around a different philosophy.

Coming from a printer that I’ve spent countless hours modifying, there’s also something quite refreshing about having a machine that is largely ready to go without needing to spend an entire weekend installing upgrades. The only thing I needed to touch was installing the 4 toolheads, removing some spacers, and hooking up the extruders with the provided PTFE tubes.

### Why the U1?

For me, the biggest selling point was the combination of multicolour capabilities, multiple materials and the tool-changing architecture. Being able to dedicate individual toolheads to different filaments opens up some interesting possibilities, particularly when working with more complex prints. One that has particularly caught my eye is TPU with PLA supports.

I’m also interested in seeing how well the machine handles everyday printing. While multicolour prints are certainly impressive, a 3D printer still needs to be reliable when printing the usual functional parts, prototypes and little engineering projects.

Ultimately, I wanted something that would let me spend less time maintaining and troubleshooting my printer, and more time actually designing and making things.

### Flexibility

One of the great things about upgrading to Klipper on the Ender 3 V2, was I could simply upload files via the web app hosted on the Raspberry Pi. The Snapmaker Orca Slicer may be the best and most refined slicer I've used. I haven't touched the main screen since I set it up, as I can simply slice my files, upload them to the printer, and monitor it, straight from my laptop. It's a seriously nice flow that I'm addicted to.

### Downsides

Apart from a few random instances where the printer fails to start a print, I'm still yet to find an issue with the machine. The only downside is the lack of a full enclosure (top cover is sold separately). I'm going to leave that upgrade for another time.

## Conclusion

Overall, the printer is really fun, and I'm still finding new features everyday!
